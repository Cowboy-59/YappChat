# Visual Direction Guide

How to write visual notes a designer or Canva can execute, diagram types and when to use each, chart selection guide, and image direction conventions.

---

## 1. Writing visual notes that execute cleanly

A visual note fails when it is vague. "Add a nice graphic here" is not a brief. "A horizontal arrow showing three steps labeled [STEP 1], [STEP 2], [STEP 3] with [PRIMARY COLOR] boxes on a white background" is a brief.

### The four-part visual note structure

1. **Shape or layout:** name the visual structure. "Two columns," "pyramid with four tiers," "circular loop with three nodes," "before-and-after comparison with a dividing line."
2. **Color instruction:** reference config colors or describe the intent. "Primary brand color for filled boxes, white text inside. Accent color for the highlighted step only."
3. **Text in the visual:** list every label, number, and caption that belongs inside the visual itself. Do not leave the designer to guess the labels.
4. **What to avoid:** state any visual choices you specifically do not want. "No gradients," "no stock photo behind the diagram," "no decorative frames."

### Example visual note (weak vs strong)

**Weak:** "Show the process visually."

**Strong:** "Horizontal left-to-right flow diagram with four steps. Step boxes are rectangles with rounded corners in [PRIMARY COLOR] background and white label text. Between each box: a right-pointing arrow in [ACCENT COLOR]. Steps are labeled: [STEP 1: Identify], [STEP 2: Prioritize], [STEP 3: Assign], [STEP 4: Review]. Below the diagram: one line of caption text: 'The decision cycle repeats weekly.' White background. No icons, no stock photos."

## 2. Diagram types and when to use each

### Process flow diagram

**When to use:** When steps have a required order and the student needs to understand the sequence.
**Common layouts:** Left-to-right linear, top-to-bottom linear, cyclical loop.
**Visual note cue:** "Horizontal arrow flow," "top-to-bottom numbered steps," "circular loop with [N] nodes."

Best for: any "how to" process, a repeating system, a step-by-step method.

---

### 2x2 matrix

**When to use:** When two dimensions determine a category, outcome, or recommendation.
**Common examples:** Urgency/importance (time management), effort/impact (prioritization), risk/reward (decisions), price/quality (competitive landscape).
**Visual note cue:** "Two-by-two grid, horizontal axis labeled [X LOW] to [X HIGH], vertical axis labeled [Y LOW] to [Y HIGH]. Four quadrant labels: [Q1 name], [Q2 name], [Q3 name], [Q4 name]."

---

### Pyramid or hierarchy

**When to use:** When elements have a ranked or foundational relationship (foundations at the bottom, advanced or rare at the top; or priorities from highest to lowest).
**Visual note cue:** "Triangle pyramid divided into [N] horizontal bands, widest at bottom. Bottom band: [FOUNDATION LABEL]. Top band: [PEAK LABEL]. Labels to the right of each band."

Best for: skill progression models, Maslow-style frameworks, priority hierarchies.

---

### Venn diagram

**When to use:** When the value is in the overlap between two or more categories.
**Visual note cue:** "Two overlapping circles. Left circle labeled [A], right circle labeled [B]. Overlap region labeled [INTERSECTION]. Color: left circle [COLOR A] at 60% opacity, right circle [COLOR B] at 60% opacity."

Best for: positioning (the overlap of skills, audience, and demand), relationship concepts, "both/and" arguments.

---

### Timeline

**When to use:** When chronological sequence or duration matters.
**Visual note cue:** "Horizontal timeline with [N] labeled milestone markers. Left end: [START LABEL]. Right end: [END LABEL]. Milestone dots in [ACCENT COLOR] with labels above or below the line."

Best for: course journeys, project phases, historical context, expected student progress over time.

---

### Comparison table

**When to use:** When the student needs to evaluate options against multiple criteria.
**Visual note cue:** "Table with [N] rows and [M] columns. First column: criteria labels. Header row: option names. Use [ACCENT COLOR] to highlight the recommended column. Checkmarks or X marks in cells where appropriate."

Best for: comparing three pricing structures, comparing lesson formats, before-and-after comparisons with multiple dimensions.

---

### Icon grid

**When to use:** When there are three to six parallel items of equal weight (no hierarchy, no sequence).
**Visual note cue:** "Grid of [N] icon-and-label pairs in [ROW x COLUMN] arrangement. Each cell: one simple icon (described below) above a short label and a one-line description. Consistent sizing, equal spacing."

Best for: a set of principles, a list of tools, a menu of options.

---

### Single-stat callout

**When to use:** When one number is the most important thing on the slide.
**Visual note cue:** "Stat in display-size type, centered, [COLOR]. Descriptor text in smaller weight below the stat. Clean background with no competing elements."

Best for: a key data point, a benchmark, a before/after metric.

## 3. Chart selection guide

| If you want to show... | Use this chart type |
|------------------------|---------------------|
| Change over time | Line chart |
| Comparison of discrete categories | Bar chart (horizontal for long labels) |
| Part-to-whole relationship | Donut or pie chart (maximum five segments) |
| Correlation between two variables | Scatter plot |
| Distribution of values | Histogram |
| A single important number | Stat callout (not a chart) |
| Ranking of items | Ranked horizontal bar chart |
| Progress toward a goal | Progress bar or gauge |

### Notes on chart design in slide decks

- Label the most important data point directly on the chart; do not make the student read a legend and cross-reference.
- Use a maximum of two colors in a chart. The important series gets the accent color; supporting series get a neutral.
- Remove gridlines if the exact values are less important than the trend or comparison.
- If the chart requires more than five seconds to interpret, simplify it or split it into two visuals.

## 4. Image direction conventions

For lessons that use photography or illustration (rather than diagrams or charts), write image direction with these conventions.

### What to specify

- **Subject:** what is depicted. "A person working at a desk, viewed from the side, natural lighting." Not "a business person."
- **Mood:** the emotional quality. "Calm and focused, not rushed or stressful." "Warm and inviting, not corporate."
- **Framing:** how the image is composed. "Horizontal, landscape orientation. Subject on the left third; right two-thirds available for text overlay."
- **What to avoid:** "No laptop on a white background. No forced smile. No generic stock-photo feel."

### Describing AI-generated images

When directing AI image generation (such as Higgsfield), describe the visual in terms of scene, lighting, composition, and mood. Do not name a specific artistic style by a living artist's name. Describe the genre instead.

Example direction for Higgsfield: "A clean, bright overhead view of a wooden desk with an open notebook, a pen, and a cup of tea. Natural daylight from the left. No people visible. Minimal and organized. Warm tones, slightly desaturated. Suitable as a background for text overlay."

### When not to use an image

Do not use a photograph on a slide if:
- It competes with on-slide text.
- It is on the slide because the slide "needs something visual" but the image adds no meaning.
- It would require the student to look at the image and read the text simultaneously.

A clean, well-typeset slide on a solid background is always better than a distracting image.
