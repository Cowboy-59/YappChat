# Slide Patterns

Full pattern library for each slide type, the five most common slide mistakes with fixes, a visual-brief writing guide, and a reference deck structure for a 10-15 minute lesson.

---

## 1. Slide type patterns

### Title slide

**Purpose:** Opens the lesson. Establishes the lesson number, the title, and the promise.

**On-slide elements:**
- Lesson number (e.g., "Lesson 2.3")
- Lesson title (the same student-facing title from the curriculum)
- Optional: the learning objective in one short sentence, or a tagline
- Optional: the brand/course name in a small footer

**What NOT to put on a title slide:** a paragraph of introduction, a long list of what will be covered, or the instructor's biography.

**Visual note template:** "[COLOR: primary brand color background or a full-bleed image that relates to the lesson theme]. Title in large white or dark text, centered or left-aligned. Lesson number in a smaller accent color above the title. No clutter."

**Speaker notes for a title slide:** Two to three sentences that function as the hook. Written as spoken words. The instructor says this while the title slide is visible; the student reads the title and hears the hook simultaneously.

---

### Objective slide

**Purpose:** States the single learning objective clearly and early. Sets the student's expectation.

**On-slide elements:**
- Headline: "By the end of this lesson, you will be able to..."
- One sentence completing the objective (Bloom's verb + specific content + context)

**Keep it to one sentence.** If two skills are being taught, it is two lessons or the objective is too broad.

**Visual note template:** "Clean, minimal background. Large body text for the objective sentence. Optional: a simple icon that relates to the skill (a tool icon, a chart icon, a person icon)."

---

### Concept slide

**Purpose:** Presents one idea from a teaching beat.

**On-slide elements:**
- Headline (the point in eight words or fewer): the main idea, not a topic label.
- Up to three supporting bullets (eight words each): the three most important things to remember about this concept.
- Nothing else.

**Headline test:** Read the headline in isolation. Does it convey the point, or does it just label the topic? "Three Types of Revenue" labels; "Not All Revenue is Equally Valuable" conveys.

**Bullet test:** Read each bullet without the headline and the speaker notes. Does it communicate something meaningful? "Increases over time" fails; "Recurring revenue compounds monthly" passes.

**Visual note template:** "Headline in large text, top third. Bullets below in medium text. Optional: one illustrative icon or small graphic on the right side. No stock photo of a person typing on a laptop."

---

### Diagram/framework slide

**Purpose:** Shows a model, a process flow, a comparison, or a structure that is easier to understand visually than in words.

**On-slide elements:**
- A headline naming the framework or model.
- The diagram itself (described in the visual note).
- Labels for each component of the diagram.
- Nothing else: no explanatory text, no paragraphs. The diagram and the speaker's narration do the explaining together.

**When to use it:** When the relationship between parts matters as much as the parts themselves (a process with steps, a matrix with four quadrants, a pyramid with tiers, a comparison of two columns).

**Visual note template (examples):**

For a process flow: "Left-to-right arrow flow with [N] labeled boxes. Each box contains a label and a one-line description. Use [PRIMARY COLOR] for boxes and [ACCENT COLOR] for arrows."

For a 2x2 matrix: "Two-by-two grid with [AXIS LABEL 1] on the horizontal axis (low left, high right) and [AXIS LABEL 2] on the vertical axis (low bottom, high top). Label each quadrant. Each quadrant has a name and a one-line descriptor. Optional: a dot or star in the 'ideal' quadrant."

For a hierarchy/pyramid: "Pyramid divided into [N] horizontal layers. Bottom layer is widest (foundation), top layer is narrowest (peak). Label each layer with its name and a three-word descriptor. Color gradient from base to top."

---

### Example slide

**Purpose:** Shows a case, a scenario, or a worked demonstration.

**On-slide elements (option A, before/after):**
- Headline: "The Difference"
- Left column label: "BEFORE" or "WITHOUT"
- Right column label: "AFTER" or "WITH"
- Two to three lines in each column (short, parallel in structure)

**On-slide elements (option B, narrative snippet):**
- Headline: the point the example illustrates
- Name and brief context (one line): "[FIRST NAME], a [ROLE]..."
- One to two sentences describing the key moment or decision
- The result (one line, bold or highlighted)

**On-slide elements (option C, step sequence):**
- Headline: the process being demonstrated
- Numbered steps, one per line, maximum five steps, maximum six words each

**Visual note for option A:** "Two-column layout with a clear dividing line. Before column: neutral or muted color. After column: accent or success color. Bold the result line."

---

### Quote or stat slide

**Purpose:** Makes a strong point land with visual emphasis.

**On-slide elements:**
- The quote or stat in large type, centered.
- Attribution in smaller text below (speaker name, source, or context).
- Nothing else.

**Use sparingly.** One or two per lesson maximum. The most important single point in the lesson deserves this treatment; a supporting detail does not.

**Visual note template:** "Full bleed background in [PRIMARY COLOR] or a high-contrast dark background. Quote in white, serif font, centered, large. Attribution in smaller weight below. No image, no border, no decoration."

---

### Practice/prompt slide

**Purpose:** Displayed while the student does the action step. It stays on screen; the instructor has paused or stepped back.

**On-slide elements:**
- Headline: "Your Turn" or the task title.
- The task instruction in two to four bullet points (complete sentences here are acceptable, because the student will read this without the instructor speaking).
- Time suggestion: "Set a timer for [X] minutes."
- A "when done" instruction: "Pause the video, complete the task, then continue."

**Visual note template:** "Clear background, slightly different from the main lesson palette to signal a shift to student work time. Instruction text in comfortable reading size. Optional: a large timer icon or a subtle progress indicator."

---

### Summary slide

**Purpose:** Closes the teaching content and reinforces the key takeaways before the action step.

**On-slide elements:**
- Headline: "What We Covered" or "Key Takeaways."
- Three to five bullets: one per teaching beat, in parallel structure.
- Bold or highlight the single most important takeaway.

**Do not add new information on a summary slide.** If a point appears here for the first time, it belongs in the lesson body, not the summary.

**Visual note template:** "Clean layout matching the concept slide style. Checkmark or dot icon before each bullet. Highlighted or bold treatment for the most important point."

---

### Action step slide

**Purpose:** Gives the student the specific task to complete. This slide must be clear enough to understand without the speaker's narration.

**On-slide elements:**
- Headline: "Before the Next Lesson" or "Your Action Step."
- The task, written as a complete instruction (full sentence).
- The output: what specifically the student produces.
- Where to save or share the output (if applicable).
- Time estimate: "(~[X] minutes)."

**Visual note template:** "Distinct visual treatment to signal this is an instruction, not new content. Consider a different background color or a checkmark/action icon. Task text in a comfortable reading size. Time estimate in accent color."

---

### Bridge slide

**Purpose:** Creates forward momentum to the next lesson. Ends on anticipation, not a flat close.

**On-slide elements:**
- Headline: "Up Next" or "Coming in Lesson [N]."
- One sentence: what the next lesson delivers and why the student will want it.

**Visual note template:** "Minimal. Matches the lesson style but with a forward-momentum feel (an arrow, a preview thumbnail of the next lesson if available, or simply a clean right-aligned design)."

## 2. The five most common slide mistakes

### Mistake 1: Too much text

**Symptom:** Slides have paragraphs or more than five bullet points. The student spends more time reading than listening.

**Why it happens:** The creator is using the slides as their notes, not as a visual companion to their voice.

**Fix:** Move everything except the headline and three essential bullets to the speaker notes. The slide is for the student's eye; the notes are for the instructor's mouth. If the slide has the same information as the speaker notes, one of them is redundant.

---

### Mistake 2: Generic visuals

**Symptom:** Every slide has a stock photo of a person at a laptop, or a blue gradient background, or a decorative swirl that has nothing to do with the content.

**Why it happens:** Filling visual space with something that looks designed is easier than thinking about what visual would actually clarify the concept.

**Fix:** Use visuals only when they add meaning (a diagram that shows a relationship, a chart that shows a data point, an icon that reinforces a label). When there is no meaningful visual, use clean typography on a solid background. Negative space is better than a distracting stock image.

---

### Mistake 3: Title-label headlines

**Symptom:** Headlines say "Module Overview," "The Framework," "Key Points," "Summary." They label what is on the slide instead of stating what matters about it.

**Why it happens:** Instructors default to topic labels because they feel neutral and safe.

**Fix:** Every headline should communicate the point, not the category. "Why Most Campaigns Fail in Week Two" is better than "Campaign Challenges." "The Rule That Changes Everything" is better than "Key Principle." Rewrite every label-headline as a point-headline.

---

### Mistake 4: Inconsistent visual treatment

**Symptom:** Each slide looks different: different font sizes, different layouts, different color usage. The deck feels assembled, not designed.

**Why it happens:** Building slides one at a time without a template or style guide.

**Fix:** Define three to four master layouts before building: title, concept, diagram, and action step. Build every slide from one of these layouts. Vary only the content, not the structure. Use `tools.design` or a simple template in any presentation tool to enforce consistency.

---

### Mistake 5: The action step buried in narration

**Symptom:** The action step is mentioned verbally in the speaker notes but does not appear as its own slide. When the student returns to the course, they cannot find what they were supposed to do.

**Why it happens:** The creator treats the action step as a verbal add-on rather than a deliverable for the student.

**Fix:** Every lesson ends with a dedicated action step slide. The instruction must be complete and readable without the audio. The student should be able to pause on this slide, read it, do the task, and continue without needing to replay the lesson.

---

## 3. Visual-brief writing guide

A visual note must be specific enough for anyone to execute it. Follow this structure for each visual note.

**Element 1: Layout descriptor.** Name the spatial arrangement: "two-column layout," "pyramid with three tiers," "left-to-right horizontal flow," "full-bleed background with centered text," "grid of four icons."

**Element 2: Color instruction.** Reference the brand colors from config or describe the intent: "primary brand color for the background, white text," "use accent color to highlight the third step only," "monochrome with one orange emphasis element."

**Element 3: Typography instruction (if non-standard).** "Headline in extra-large weight," "stat in display size, center of screen," "caption below the chart in small italic."

**Element 4: Content placeholders.** List what goes in each area: "[LABEL 1] top left, [LABEL 2] bottom right, [ARROW] connecting them with the label [CONNECTION NAME]."

**Element 5: What to avoid.** "No stock photos," "no decorative borders," "no more than three colors in this diagram."

## 4. Reference deck structure for a 10-15 minute lesson

This is the standard 12-16 slide structure for a single video lesson of 10-15 minutes.

| Slide | Type | Purpose |
|-------|------|---------|
| 1 | Title | Lesson number, title, visual hook |
| 2 | Objective | Single learning objective |
| 3 | Concept | Beat 1: main point |
| 4 | Diagram or example | Illustrate Beat 1 |
| 5 | Concept | Beat 2: main point |
| 6 | Example | Illustrate Beat 2 (the anchor example) |
| 7 | Concept | Beat 3: main point |
| 8 | Diagram or framework | Illustrate Beat 3 |
| 9 | Concept (optional) | Beat 4 if the lesson has one |
| 10 | Example or comparison | Illustrate Beat 4 (optional) |
| 11 | Summary | Key takeaways, three to five bullets |
| 12 | Practice prompt | Action step instruction (complete, readable alone) |
| 13 | Bridge | One sentence to the next lesson |

Adjust for shorter lessons by removing optional Beat 4 slides. Adjust for longer lessons by adding a second diagram or a worked example between beats. Do not exceed 18 slides for a single 15-minute lesson; more slides suggest the content should be split.
