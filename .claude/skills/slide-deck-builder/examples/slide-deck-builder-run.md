# Slide Deck Builder: Worked Example

Sample brand (for illustration only): Bram van der Berg is a product manager who teaches a course called "PM Fundamentals for Engineers." This is the slide deck brief for Lesson 3.1: "The Three Questions Every PM Asks Before Building Anything."

---

## Inputs

- Lesson title: The Three Questions Every PM Asks Before Building Anything
- Learning objective: Apply the three PM pre-build questions to a provided feature request and produce a decision rationale in writing.
- Lesson type: Concept + worked example
- Key beats:
  1. Most teams start building too early because "done" feels like progress.
  2. The three questions (Who has this problem? How often? What happens if we don't solve it?) filter 80% of low-value work.
  3. A worked example: the "dark mode" feature request.
- Target length: 11 minutes
- Format: Recorded video lesson for a self-paced course.

**Slide 3: Concept - Beat 1**

On-slide text:
- Headline: "Building Is Not Progress"
- Bullets:
  - "Teams confuse activity with value."
  - "Shipping without a decision rationale is expensive."
  - "The cost arrives months later."

Speaker notes: "Here's the trap. When a feature request comes in, the most natural thing to do is start designing it. That feels productive. But starting too early is the most common reason PMs end up on teams that are always busy and rarely moving the needle. The cost of building the wrong thing is not the build time; it's the opportunity cost of everything you didn't build instead."

Visual note: White background. Headline in large bold text, top quarter of the slide. Three bullets in medium text below, left-aligned with a simple dash or dot. No image. A subtle red or orange accent on the word "Not" in the headline, or on the third bullet to emphasize the delayed cost.

Speaker notes: "Before any feature goes on the roadmap, every PM should be able to answer these three questions. Not from memory, not as an assumption: with actual evidence. Let me walk you through each one."

**Slide 5: Concept - Question 1**

On-slide text:
- Headline: "Who Has This Problem?"
- Bullets:
  - "'Everyone' is not an answer."
  - "Name a specific segment and size it."
  - "Vague audience = unfocused feature."

Speaker notes: "The first question sounds obvious, but in practice it's the one most teams skip. When someone asks for dark mode, the instinct is to say 'users who work at night' or even 'everyone.' Neither of those is an answer you can build for. You need a specific segment: enterprise customers who use the product in low-light environments for more than two hours a day. Now you have something you can size, qualify, and prioritize."

Visual note: White background. Headline in large bold. Bullets in medium text. Optional: a simple icon of a silhouette or a target to the right, reinforcing the idea of specificity. Keep it minimal.

---

**Slide 6: Concept - Question 2**

On-slide text:
- Headline: "How Often Does It Happen?"
- Bullets:
  - "Daily pain vs rare edge case."
  - "Frequency determines urgency."
  - "Ask for actual usage data."

Speaker notes: "The second question is about frequency, and it is a filter. A problem that affects 200 users every day is very different from a problem that affects 2,000 users once a year. Both are real problems; they are not equally important. When you ask 'how often,' you are separating the urgent from the nice-to-have. And the honest answer should come from data, not from the loudest voice in the last customer meeting."

Visual note: White background. Same layout as Slide 5. Optional: a simple bar chart icon or a frequency-scale illustration showing "rarely" on the left and "daily" on the right, with a marker in the middle. Clean and minimal.

---

**Slide 7: Concept - Question 3**

On-slide text:
- Headline: "What Happens If We Don't Fix It?"
- Bullets:
  - "Churn? Revenue lost? Support tickets?"
  - "No clear answer = low priority."
  - "Quantify the cost of inaction."

Speaker notes: "This is the question that separates a PM from an order-taker. If you can't describe what happens to the business if this problem stays unsolved, you don't have enough information to prioritize it. Maybe the answer is 'we lose three enterprise renewals a year.' Maybe it's 'nothing, users find a workaround in 30 seconds.' Both of those answers change where the feature sits on the roadmap. Get the answer before you put it in the queue."

Visual note: White background. Same layout as Slides 5 and 6 for visual consistency. Optional: a simple "cost" or "impact" icon. Keep the three concept slides visually parallel so the student's eye knows what to expect.

**Slide 8: Example - The Dark Mode Request**

On-slide text:
- Headline: "Dark Mode: A Real Question Trio"
- Two-column comparison:
  - Left column header: "WITHOUT THE QUESTIONS"
  - Right column header: "WITH THE QUESTIONS"

Speaker notes: "Let me show you how this plays out on a real feature request. Dark mode comes up constantly. Here's what the conversation looks like before and after the three questions."

Visual note: [DESIGN NEEDED] Two-column layout with a thin dividing line. Left column (WITHOUT): muted gray or red-tinted background for the column. Content: "Request: 'Can we add dark mode?' Decision: Let's add it to Q3 roadmap." Right column (WITH): white or green-tinted background. Content: "Q1: Who? Power users in the analytics module, ~400 accounts. Q2: How often? Daily for 2+ hours. Q3: What happens without it? Eye strain complaints; three cancellation mentions in NPS comments." Bold the last item in the right column. The point is visible: the right column has evidence; the left column has an assumption.

---

**Slide 9: Worked Example - Decision Rationale**

On-slide text:
- Headline: "The Decision Rationale (Written)"
- Body (three lines, normal paragraph size): "Prioritize: yes. Segment: Analytics power users. Evidence: Daily use, eye strain NPS comments, three cancellation references."

Speaker notes: "Here is what a written decision rationale looks like for dark mode. Three lines. Prioritize: yes or no. Segment: who specifically. Evidence: why. This is what you put in the roadmap card, not 'users want it.' A rationale this specific takes two minutes to write and saves hours of debate in the planning meeting."

Visual note: White background. Headline at the top. A simple card or document shape containing the three-line rationale, with a slight drop shadow or border to make it look like a physical card or record. Clean, easy to read.

**Slide 10: Summary**

On-slide text:
- Headline: "The Three Pre-Build Questions"
- Bullets:
  - "Who has this problem? (name a segment)"
  - "How often? (daily vs rare)"
  - "What happens without a fix? (quantify)"
- Bold line: "No answer? Not ready for the roadmap."

Speaker notes: "Three questions. Every feature, every time. You don't need a perfect answer; you need a defensible one. If you can't answer any one of the three, the feature is not ready to build. Put it back in discovery."

Visual note: [PRIMARY COLOR] background, white text. Bullets with the numbered question framework icons from Slide 4, scaled small. The bold line at the bottom in accent color or slightly larger weight for emphasis.
