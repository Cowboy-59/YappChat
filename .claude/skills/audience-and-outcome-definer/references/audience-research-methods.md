# Audience Research Methods

How to gather primary evidence for audience profiles, and how to build a strong profile when research is limited.

---

## 1. Primary research methods

### Method 1: Community listening

The fastest method when you do not yet have an audience. Spend 30-60 minutes reading forums, groups, and comment sections where your target student congregates.

What to look for:
- Questions asked repeatedly (the problem is real and widespread).
- Language used to describe the problem (not expert language; the words the audience actually uses).
- Failed solutions mentioned ("I tried X and it didn't work because...").
- Desired outcomes stated directly ("I just want to be able to...").
- Emotions: frustration, embarrassment, confusion, hope.

Where to look (generic categories, not specific platforms):
- Topic-specific subreddits or forum communities.
- Professional association discussion boards.
- YouTube comment sections on popular tutorial videos in your niche.
- Questions posted in Facebook groups for your audience type.
- LinkedIn posts or comment threads where practitioners discuss their challenges.
- Amazon reviews of books in your category (especially the 3-star reviews, which are detailed and honest).

What to capture: copy the exact language used, not a paraphrase. The audience's words become your sales copy, your lesson titles, and your outcome statements.

### Method 2: Pre-launch survey

Run before building the course. Keep it short (five to seven questions maximum). Share in communities or with your existing audience.

Recommended questions:

1. "What is the number one challenge you face with [TOPIC]?" (Open-ended. Collect exact language.)
2. "What have you already tried to solve this? What happened?" (Reveals failed alternatives and helps position the course.)
3. "If you could achieve one specific result with [TOPIC] in the next 90 days, what would it be?" (Reveals desired outcome in their words.)
4. "Have you ever paid for a course, book, or coaching on [TOPIC]? If yes, what was missing?" (Price tolerance and product gaps.)
5. "What is your current experience level with [TOPIC]?" (Calibrates course level.)
6. "What would you need to see to feel confident paying [PRICE] for a course on this?" (Surfaces objections and proof requirements.)

Aim for at least 20-30 responses before drawing conclusions.

### Method 3: One-on-one interviews

The richest method. Five to ten 20-minute conversations reveal more than 200 survey responses.

Who to talk to: people who match your target student profile, including those who have already tried to solve the problem and either succeeded or failed.

Interview structure:

1. "Tell me about your current situation with [TOPIC]." (Let them talk. Do not interrupt.)
2. "What does that make harder or more frustrating?" (Dig into the pain.)
3. "What have you tried to fix it? What happened?" (Prior attempts.)
4. "If this were solved, what would be different for you?" (The after state in their words.)
5. "What is stopping you from solving it right now?" (Objections and constraints.)
6. "Have you paid for help with this before? What was that like?" (Buying history.)

What to do with the output: transcribe or note the exact language. Look for patterns across interviews. Three or more people using the same phrase about the same frustration is a finding, not a coincidence.

## 2. How to build a profile without primary research

Not everyone can run surveys or interviews before launching. When research is limited, use these approaches in order of reliability.

### Approach 1: Use your own experience

If the creator is teaching something they themselves struggled to learn, their own journey is a proxy for the audience's journey. Write the profile as if describing your past self at the moment you most needed the solution.

Be specific: "I was three years into freelancing and still taking any project that came my way because I was afraid to niche down. I had tried reading business books and taking a general marketing course. Neither told me how to actually say no to a bad-fit client." This level of specificity is useful. "I was struggling with freelancing" is not.

### Approach 2: Use the course-validator community evidence

If the course-validator skill was run first, the community-evidence section should include specific forum types or community behaviors. Expand those notes into a profile by extrapolating: if people are posting "how do I do X without Y," the before state includes the constraint Y and the goal includes the task X.

### Approach 3: Derive from the outcome

Work backwards from what the course teaches. If the course teaches a specific skill at an intermediate level, the student profile follows:
- They already have basic exposure (or the course would start at beginner level).
- They are stuck at a specific gap (the problem the intermediate skill solves).
- They are motivated to close the gap (or they would not seek an intermediate course).

The profile is the before state that makes the outcome meaningful.

### Approach 4: Use analogous audiences

If your exact audience has not been researched, find a closely related audience that has. A course for veterinary receptionists on client communication can borrow from research on medical office staff communication, adjusting for the specific vocabulary and context.

Label this clearly: "Based on analogous research in [RELATED FIELD]. Validate with direct audience conversations before finalizing."

## 3. Synthesizing research into a profile

When you have collected evidence (survey responses, interview notes, community quotes), run this synthesis process.

1. **Extract phrases.** Collect all the distinct phrases your research produced. Copy them exactly.
2. **Cluster by theme.** Group phrases by the type of signal: problem descriptions, failed solutions, desired outcomes, emotional language, vocabulary.
3. **Identify the dominant pattern.** Which problem appeared most often? Which phrase captured it best? That is your before state and your audience vocabulary.
4. **Write the portrait.** Draft the five-dimension portrait (situation, pain, prior attempts, goal, vocabulary) using the most representative phrases. Write in second person.
5. **Test the portrait.** Read it to one person who matches the profile. Ask: "Does this sound like you?" If they say yes, you have a strong profile. If they correct something, update it.

---

## 4. Audience validation questions

Before finalizing the audience portrait, answer these:

- Can you name three specific people who match this profile? (Real people you know, not a demographic abstraction.)
- Would this student recognize themselves in the "not for you" list? (If not, the list is too vague.)
- Does the vocabulary section contain at least two phrases you heard from a real person in this audience?
- Is the before state something the student would describe as painful today, not someday?
- Is the after state something the student is willing to pay for right now, not just hoping for abstractly?

If any answer is no, the profile needs more specificity before the curriculum is designed around it.
