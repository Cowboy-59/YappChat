# Workbook Design Principles

How to write instructions students actually follow, output placeholder design, reading level calibration, and a quality rubric for evaluating workbook exercises.

---

## 1. Writing instructions students actually follow

The most common reason a workbook exercise goes uncompleted is that the instructions are unclear, intimidating, or feel like extra work rather than a step in the learning process.

### Principle 1: One task per exercise

If an exercise has two distinct tasks, it has two exercises. Combining them increases cognitive load and reduces completion rates. "Write your answer to Question A and then evaluate it against the rubric" is two exercises poorly stapled together.

### Principle 2: Start with a verb

Every instruction opens with an action verb that is specific and immediate. "Consider the following..." does not start the student. "Write down the three..." does.

Strong openers: "List," "Write," "Fill in," "Rate," "Circle," "Identify," "Draft," "Complete," "Choose," "Describe."

Weak openers: "Consider," "Think about," "Reflect on," "Take a moment to," "It may be helpful to."

### Principle 3: Tell the student what done looks like

Specify the format, length, or quantity. "Write two to three sentences" is clearer than "write a short response." "Fill in all four rows of the table below" is clearer than "complete the table."

### Principle 4: Calibrate the difficulty to the moment

An exercise in Lesson 1 of Module 1 should be completable by anyone who watched the lesson, without additional research or external tools. An exercise in Lesson 5 of Module 3 can require integration and judgment, because the student has more context. Do not ask for sophisticated analysis before the student has the tools for it.

### Principle 5: Make the stakes feel real but manageable

Frame exercises around the student's own situation where possible. "Apply this to your own [PROJECT/BUSINESS/SITUATION]" is more motivating than "fill in this hypothetical." At the same time, a blank "your own situation" prompt with no structure is intimidating. Pair the personal frame with a template or a structured placeholder.

## 2. Output placeholder design

A placeholder is what appears in the worksheet where the student writes their answer. Poorly designed placeholders are the second most common reason exercises go uncompleted.

### Placeholder design rules

**Rule 1: Match the placeholder to the output type.**

| Output type | Placeholder format |
|-------------|-------------------|
| A word or phrase | A short blank line with a label: "My choice: _____" |
| A sentence or two | A box with a label and a gentle size constraint: "2-3 sentences" |
| A list | Numbered or bulleted lines with enough items for the expected output: five blank bullet lines for a "list five" exercise |
| A table | A pre-built table with column headers and the right number of rows |
| A longer written response | A large box labeled "Your response:" with optional line guides |
| A drawing or diagram | A framed blank space labeled "Sketch your diagram here:" |

**Rule 2: Label every placeholder.**

A blank box with no label is ambiguous. "Your answer:" tells the student it is their turn but nothing else. "Your three pricing options:" tells the student exactly what belongs in the space.

**Rule 3: Size the placeholder appropriately.**

A placeholder that is too small signals to the student that a short answer is expected, even when it is not. A placeholder that is too large (a full page for a two-sentence response) creates anxiety about filling space. In digital workbooks, size boxes generously. In print, label the expected length explicitly ("2-4 sentences") so the student is not intimidated by blank space.

**Rule 4: Do not over-scaffold.**

A worksheet that fills in so much for the student that there is almost nothing left to do is not an exercise; it is an example with the student's name added. Scaffolding should lower activation energy, not eliminate thinking. The student should still make a real decision or produce an original output.

## 3. Reading level calibration

The workbook should be readable by the target student without extra effort. Difficult instructions increase cognitive load and reduce completion.

### Reading level by audience type

| Audience | Reading level target | What it means in practice |
|----------|--------------------|-----------------------------|
| Beginners, general public | Grade 8-10 | Short sentences, active voice, no jargon without definition |
| Professionals in their field | Grade 10-12 | Technical vocabulary is fine; avoid academic or legalese constructions |
| Advanced practitioners | College level | Assume vocabulary; focus on precision |

### Practical calibration tips

- **Sentence length:** Aim for an average of 15-20 words per sentence in the instructions. Longer sentences can be trimmed by splitting at conjunctions.
- **Vocabulary:** Avoid words the target student would need to look up. If a technical term must appear, define it inline the first time.
- **Active voice:** "Rate each option on the scale below" (active) instead of "Each option should be rated on the scale below" (passive).
- **Second person:** Address the student directly as "you." Never use "the learner" or "students" in the workbook itself; it creates distance.
- **Sentence variety:** Vary sentence length. A worksheet that reads as a uniform stream of 15-word sentences feels monotonous. Short sentences for instructions. A slightly longer sentence for context when needed.

## 4. Quality rubric for workbook exercises

Rate each exercise on four dimensions before delivering.

### Dimension 1: Output specificity (1-3)

| Score | What it means |
|-------|---------------|
| 3 | The output placeholder tells the student exactly what format and scope their answer should take. A student who completes the exercise correctly could not produce the same output as a different student (it is personal to their situation). |
| 2 | The output format is clear, but the scope is somewhat vague, or the exercise could be completed generically. |
| 1 | The output is a vague reflection ("think about your situation") with no specific format or deliverable. |

### Dimension 2: Connection to learning objective (1-3)

| Score | What it means |
|-------|---------------|
| 3 | Completing the exercise requires the student to use the method, framework, or skill from the lesson's learning objective. Finishing the exercise is equivalent to achieving the objective at a practiced level. |
| 2 | The exercise relates to the lesson but does not directly require the skill to be applied; a student could complete it without using what they learned. |
| 1 | The exercise is tangentially related to the lesson or addresses a different topic. |

### Dimension 3: Completion ease (1-3)

| Score | What it means |
|-------|---------------|
| 3 | A motivated student who watched the lesson could complete this exercise in 10-30 minutes without additional resources. Instructions are clear, placeholders are labeled, and the expected output is explicit. |
| 2 | The exercise is completable but requires re-watching part of the lesson or looking something up. |
| 1 | The exercise is confusing, assumes knowledge not covered in the lesson, or has no clear success state. |

### Dimension 4: Retention value (1-3)

| Score | What it means |
|-------|---------------|
| 3 | Completing this exercise meaningfully increases the student's ability to recall and apply the lesson content later. The output is something they will refer back to. |
| 2 | The exercise is useful but produces a throwaway output (a quick reflection that is not saved or used in later lessons). |
| 1 | The exercise is busywork: it takes time but does not deepen understanding or produce a usable artifact. |

**Total: 12 = excellent, ready to include. 9-11 = good, minor revision may strengthen it. 6-8 = needs revision on the lowest dimension. Below 6 = replace or redesign.**

## 5. Module workbook completion rate factors

These are the factors most commonly associated with high completion rates across self-paced course workbooks.

| Factor | High-completion version | Low-completion version |
|--------|------------------------|----------------------|
| Exercise length | Most exercises complete in under 20 minutes | Many exercises take more than 30 minutes |
| Instructions | Specific, action-verb first, explicit output | Vague, reflective, no clear done state |
| Personal relevance | Student applies to their own situation | Student completes a generic scenario |
| Progress visibility | Checkbox list of lessons; named completion marker at end | No visible progress indicators |
| Capstone motivation | End-of-module deliverable is genuinely useful outside the course | End-of-module deliverable is just a summary |
| Format accessibility | Can be completed in a PDF tool, Google Doc, or print | Requires a specific tool the student may not have |
| Early wins | First exercise in the module is completable in under 10 minutes | First exercise is complex or intimidating |
