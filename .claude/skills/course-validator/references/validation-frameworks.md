# Validation Frameworks

Deep reference for the course-validator skill. Load when the task calls for more precision in scoring, gap analysis, or promise assessment.

---

## 1. Demand-signal scoring rubric

Score each signal 1-5. Average the three for the demand dimension score.

### Signal A: Search intent

| Score | What it means |
|-------|---------------|
| 5 | The exact problem has multiple high-volume search phrases. Audiences type it as a question or a "how to." Example: "how to read financial statements as a beginner," "freelance proposal template." |
| 4 | Strong search phrases exist but are slightly general. The creator will need to niche down the title to capture intent. |
| 3 | The problem is searched, but the phrases are broad or competitive. Discoverability requires a specific angle. |
| 2 | Low search volume. The problem is real but audiences do not know to search for a solution yet. |
| 1 | No meaningful search intent. The idea lives in the creator's head, not in the audience's vocabulary. |

**How to use it:** Ask the user to describe how their audience would type the problem into a search bar. If they struggle to produce 3 specific phrases, the score is 2 or below.

### Signal B: Community evidence

| Score | What it means |
|-------|---------------|
| 5 | Active communities where this exact problem surfaces repeatedly. Members share failed attempts, ask for recommendations, celebrate solutions. |
| 4 | Communities exist and the problem appears, but not as a dominant recurring thread. |
| 3 | Adjacent communities exist. The problem appears, but framed differently from how the course frames it. |
| 2 | Communities exist for the audience, but the problem is not a visible pain point in them. |
| 1 | No community evidence. The creator assumes the audience has this problem without external confirmation. |

**Calibration example (score 4):** A course on "writing better Slack messages for remote teams" would find community evidence in remote-work forums and manager communities, but fewer dedicated threads specifically about Slack writing quality. Score 4, not 5.

**Calibration example (score 2):** A course on "advanced competitive moat analysis for indie game studios." The audience exists but the specific problem has almost no community surface area. Score 2.

### Signal C: Willingness to pay

| Score | What it means |
|-------|---------------|
| 5 | This audience actively buys courses, coaching, or tools at the proposed price or higher. Direct evidence exists (competing paid products, community mentions of having purchased similar training). |
| 4 | The audience buys training but typically at a lower price. The proposed price requires a strong promise or added support. |
| 3 | Mixed signals. Some members buy; many expect free content. |
| 2 | The audience is price-resistant or primarily consumes free content. Selling requires significant trust-building. |
| 1 | No buying history. The audience has never purchased training in this category. Starting with a free or very low-cost entry offer is advisable. |

## 2. Competitive-gap analysis method

The goal is not to find a market with no competition (that usually means no demand), but to find the specific gap your course fills better than the alternatives.

### The three-archetype map

For every validation, identify one example of each archetype (use generic descriptors, not brand names):

**Archetype 1: The free alternative**
What free content exists on YouTube, blogs, or podcasts? What does it teach well, and where does it fall short? Free content usually covers "what" and "why" but not "how" in a sequenced, accountable format.

**Archetype 2: The low-price competitor**
A $27-$197 self-study course. Usually comprehensive in volume but weak on transformation support, community, or accountability. Often broad and unfocused.

**Archetype 3: The premium competitor**
A $500+ course, coaching program, or cohort. Usually strong on transformation but requires significant time commitment and price. Often out of reach for beginners.

### Locating the gap

After mapping all three archetypes, ask: what does the ideal student still not have? Common gap types:

| Gap type | Description | Example |
|----------|-------------|---------|
| Specificity gap | Existing options are too broad for a defined sub-audience | "All copywriting courses cover everything; nobody teaches specifically for SaaS landing pages" |
| Proof gap | Existing options lack credible results or case studies | "Courses exist but instructors have no verifiable track record in the topic" |
| Speed gap | Existing options require too much time to see results | "The best resources take 12+ months; my audience needs a 30-day result" |
| Support gap | Existing options are all self-study with no community or feedback | "Everyone learns in isolation; no course offers live critique" |
| Format gap | Existing options do not match how this audience prefers to learn | "All courses are video-heavy; this audience needs text-first with quick reference sheets" |
| Price gap | The market has only free and very expensive; the mid-tier is empty | "There is YouTube content and a $2,000 coaching program; nothing in the $200-$500 range" |

**A strong gap statement:** "For [AUDIENCE], existing options are [too broad / too expensive / too slow / too unsupported]. [COURSE TITLE] fills this by [specific differentiator]."

## 3. Promise-strength criteria

A strong course promise passes all four tests.

### Test 1: Specificity

**Weak:** "You'll improve your communication skills."
**Strong:** "You'll write email responses that resolve client complaints in one exchange, without sounding defensive or losing the relationship."

Specificity checklist:
- Does it name a measurable change in behavior or output?
- Does it specify the context in which the student applies the skill?
- Does it avoid words like "better," "more confident," and "deeper understanding"?

### Test 2: Achievability within the course

**Weak:** "You'll master advanced data science" (requires years of practice beyond any course).
**Strong:** "You'll build and deploy your first machine learning model to a live URL" (completable in a course sprint).

Test: Can a motivated student complete the transformation in the course's timeframe? If the answer requires "and then another year of practice," the promise is too large. Narrow the scope.

### Test 3: Creator credibility

Ask: has the creator personally achieved the outcome they are teaching? Do they have documented results (clients, case studies, their own portfolio)?

| Credibility level | What it means for the course |
|-------------------|-------------------------------|
| Direct experience | Creator has done the thing and can show results. Strong foundation. |
| Adjacent experience | Creator has related expertise. Needs a credibility bridge: co-teacher, case study partners, or documented research. |
| Aspiring expertise | Creator is learning the topic themselves. Transparent positioning required. May need to launch a lower-stakes beta first. |

### Test 4: Price-to-outcome alignment

The perceived value of the outcome must be clearly greater than the course price. A useful test:

- If the student applies the outcome, what is it worth to them in time saved, money earned, or problem solved?
- Is that value at least 10x the course price?

If the answer is "maybe 3-5x," the price is too high, the promise needs to be stronger, or the bonuses need to close the gap.

## 4. Creator-fit assessment guide

Three questions to surface gaps before launch.

**Question 1: Can you teach it?**
Does the creator have both the knowledge and the ability to explain it clearly to a beginner? Teaching ability is separate from expertise. A brilliant practitioner who cannot explain their process clearly will produce a frustrating course.

**Question 2: Can you prove it?**
Proof takes many forms: personal results, client results, case studies, testimonials, or a documented methodology. Rank the strength:
- Tier 1: Your own results, verifiable and specific.
- Tier 2: Client or student results with permission to cite.
- Tier 3: A documented framework you have tested, even without public results.
- Tier 4: Research or synthesis from other sources, with clear attribution.

Tier 4 alone is weak for a paid course. Aim for at least Tier 3 with a path to Tier 2 after the first beta cohort.

**Question 3: Can you show up for it?**
Will the creator still be energized to teach this topic in six months? A course takes sustained effort to record, market, and support. Pick a topic that the creator genuinely finds interesting to discuss, not just commercially attractive.

## 5. Verdict scorecard calibration

How to assign the final 1-5 scores for each lens.

| Lens | Score 5 | Score 3 | Score 1 |
|------|---------|---------|---------|
| Demand | All three signals strong; audience is actively searching and buying | Two signals moderate; one weak; audience exists but buying behavior uncertain | No meaningful demand signals; problem is assumed, not confirmed |
| Competition | Clear gap identified; differentiator is specific and defensible | Some gap exists but competitors are close; differentiation requires price or angle adjustment | Saturated with no clear gap, or no competition at all (suggesting no demand) |
| Promise | Specific, achievable, credibly deliverable, and worth 10x the price | Two of four promise tests pass cleanly; two are borderline | Vague outcome, impossible to achieve in a course, or price-to-value is inverted |
| Fit | Direct experience, verifiable proof, and genuine interest | Adjacent experience and some proof; energy is moderate | No direct experience, no proof, and low enthusiasm |

### Aggregate verdict rules

| Aggregate pattern | Verdict |
|-------------------|---------|
| All four scores 3 or above, no score below 3 | Go (note any 3s as watch points) |
| One score at 2 or below | Refine: fix the weak lens before building |
| Two or more scores at 2 or below, or any score at 1 | Stop and rethink: significant repositioning needed |

## 6. Five competitive-gap worked examples

**Example 1: Freelance financial management for creative professionals**
- Free alternative: generic personal finance YouTube content. Good on budgeting basics; ignores irregular income, quarterly taxes, or creative-industry contracts.
- Low-price: a $97 freelancer finance course. Broad; covers all freelancers; uses examples from tech, not creatives.
- Premium: a $3,000 business-finance coaching program. Strong but out of budget for early-career creatives.
- Gap type: Specificity gap. Course angle: "financial systems built specifically for designers, photographers, and illustrators with variable monthly income."

**Example 2: Email writing for customer support teams**
- Free alternative: general business writing guides and blog posts. Cover tone and grammar; skip de-escalation, resolution speed, and brand voice consistency.
- Low-price: a $47 business email course. Focused on sales; support scenarios are a single module at the end.
- Premium: a $1,500 customer experience program. Comprehensive but overkill for a support writer who just wants better email skills.
- Gap type: Specificity gap plus format gap. Course angle: "quick-reference email playbook for support reps; each scenario covered in under 10 minutes."

**Example 3: Sourdough bread for apartment bakers**
- Free alternative: abundant YouTube content. Excellent technique videos but assumes a stand mixer, a Dutch oven, and ample counter space.
- Low-price: a $39 sourdough starter course. Great for the early steps but stops before advanced shaping and troubleshooting.
- Premium: a $400 artisan baking course. Comprehensive and beautiful but designed for home bakers with full kitchens.
- Gap type: Specificity gap. Course angle: "sourdough in a small kitchen with no special equipment; adapted for apartment constraints."

**Example 4: Client onboarding systems for coaches**
- Free alternative: generic client management blog posts. Cover CRMs and contracts at a surface level; ignore the coaching-specific nuances of intake, boundaries, and transformation tracking.
- Low-price: a $127 systems course for service businesses. Covers all service types; coaching applications are assumed, not shown.
- Premium: a $2,000 business-building coaching program. Includes client onboarding as one of 12 modules; not the focus.
- Gap type: Specificity and depth gap. Course angle: "a complete onboarding system designed specifically for coaches, covering intake forms, first-session protocols, and progress-tracking tools."

**Example 5: Intro to machine learning for marketing analysts**
- Free alternative: strong university-style ML courses on free platforms. Excellent depth but require Python fluency and a math background that most marketing analysts lack.
- Low-price: a $79 no-code ML tool tutorial. Practical but teaches one tool, not the underlying thinking.
- Premium: a $1,200 data science bootcamp. Full curriculum but six months long; built for career changers, not practicing analysts.
- Gap type: Audience fit and speed gap. Course angle: "ML concepts and practical models for marketing analysts using spreadsheet-first tools; no Python required."
