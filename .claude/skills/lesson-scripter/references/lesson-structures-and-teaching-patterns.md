# Lesson Structures and Teaching Patterns

Detailed scripts for each lesson type, hook formulas, explanation patterns, and common script failure modes. Load when writing or reviewing a lesson script.

---

## 1. The five lesson type scripts

### Type 1: Concept lesson script structure

A concept lesson introduces a new mental model or framework. The risk is that it becomes a lecture. Anchor it with examples and keep the beats tight.

**Script structure:**
1. Hook (problem or curiosity hook works best for concept lessons).
2. Objective statement.
3. Beat 1: Name the concept and explain what it is.
4. Beat 2: Explain what problem it solves or why it matters.
5. Beat 3: Show the concept in action (anchor example).
6. Beat 4 (optional): Common misconceptions or edge cases.
7. Summary and single key takeaway.
8. Action step (often a reflection or a categorization exercise).
9. Bridge to next lesson.

**Common mistake:** Explaining the concept three times in different words instead of advancing to an example. Once the student understands the definition, move to the application immediately.

---

### Type 2: Demonstration lesson script structure

A demonstration lesson shows the skill being performed. The instructor is the model; the student observes and then imitates.

**Script structure:**
1. Hook (promise hook works best: state what they will see demonstrated and why it matters).
2. Objective statement.
3. Beat 1: Brief setup (what we are about to do and why this specific approach).
4. Beat 2: First step of the demonstration, narrated in real time with decision rationale. "I'm doing X because Y."
5. Beat 3: Second step, narrated. [SHOW] markers for the screen or visual.
6. Beat 4: Third step or completion of the demonstration.
7. Beat 5 (optional): Replay of the key decision point with an explanation of what would happen if the wrong choice were made.
8. Summary: name the three or four most important decisions the student just observed.
9. Action step: replicate the demonstration on their own content or materials.
10. Bridge to the practice lesson that follows.

**Common mistake:** Narrating what you are doing without explaining why. A student watching a demonstration needs the reasoning, not just the steps.

---

### Type 3: Worked example lesson script structure

A worked example shows a complete case from start to finish, usually a finished piece of work accompanied by a guided analysis.

**Script structure:**
1. Hook (problem hook: "Here's the mistake I see constantly, and here's a better approach side by side.").
2. Objective statement.
3. Beat 1: Introduce the example (who is the fictional person in the case, what is their situation, what is the task).
4. Beat 2: Show the weak or incomplete version first (if applicable) and name its problems.
5. Beat 3: Show the strong version and walk through each decision that makes it strong.
6. Beat 4: Extract the transferable principle (what made this case work that the student can apply to their own situation).
7. Summary.
8. Action step: apply the principle to one element of their own work and compare against the example.
9. Bridge.

**Common mistake:** Showing only the finished good example without showing the reasoning behind it. Students learn from the decision-making process, not from a finished artifact they have to reverse-engineer.

---

### Type 4: Practice lesson script structure

A practice lesson gives the student a prompt and sends them to do the work. The instruction is minimal; the doing is the point.

**Script structure:**
1. Hook (brief: connect this practice to what was just learned).
2. Objective statement.
3. Beat 1: Describe the practice task clearly (what they will do, what they will produce).
4. Beat 2: Set up any constraints or parameters (time limit, format, materials needed).
5. Beat 3: Give one worked example of the first step only (lower the activation energy; do not do the whole task for them).
6. [PAUSE marker: "Pause the video here, complete the task, then come back."]
7. Beat 4 (after the pause): Brief debrief on what good output looks like. What are the signs they are on track?
8. Action step: In a practice lesson, the practice is the action step. Note where to save or share the output.
9. Bridge.

**Common mistake:** Giving so much instruction before the practice that the student feels they have already done the lesson without actually doing anything. Keep the setup under three minutes.

---

### Type 5: Synthesis lesson script structure

A synthesis lesson combines multiple previously taught skills. It is the capstone of a module.

**Script structure:**
1. Hook (callback hook: "Over the past [N] lessons, you've learned [skill A], [skill B], and [skill C]. Today we're going to use all three at once.").
2. Objective statement.
3. Beat 1: Remind the student of the three to five skills they are combining (do not re-teach; just name and recall).
4. Beat 2: Introduce the synthesis task (the complete artifact or scenario they will tackle).
5. Beat 3: Walk through the integration strategy (what to do first, what to do second, what connects to what).
6. Beat 4: Show one example of the integration in a partial or complete worked case.
7. [PAUSE marker for the student to complete the synthesis task.]
8. Beat 5: Debrief on the completion criteria (what a finished, complete output looks like).
9. Summary: name what the student now has and why it is valuable.
10. Action step: save and review the completed synthesis artifact.
11. Bridge: frame the transition to the next module.

**Common mistake:** Re-teaching everything from previous lessons instead of trusting that the student remembers. A synthesis lesson should move quickly into the integrated task.

## 2. Hook formulas with worked examples

### Formula 1: The shared failure

"When I first tried [TOPIC], I made one mistake that cost me [CONSEQUENCE]. I'm going to show you exactly what it was so you don't make the same one."

Worked example (course on email list building):
"When I first set up my email opt-in, I used a generic 'join my newsletter' button and got almost zero sign-ups for three months. Not because my content was bad, but because I was asking for trust without giving any reason to say yes. In this lesson I'm going to show you the four-word formula that changes that entirely."

---

### Formula 2: The counterintuitive fact

"Most people believe [COMMON BELIEF]. Here's why that's exactly backwards, and what actually works."

Worked example (course on public speaking):
"Most people believe that the way to calm nerves before presenting is to think about the audience. Focus on them, not yourself. And that advice is technically right, but it almost always makes things worse because it shifts your attention to what they might be thinking about you. What actually works is the exact opposite, and in this lesson I'll show you what to do instead."

---

### Formula 3: The specific promise

"By the end of this lesson, you'll have [SPECIFIC DELIVERABLE] that you can use [IMMEDIATELY OR BY WHEN]. This is the part most [AUDIENCE TYPE] skip, which is exactly why they keep [FRUSTRATION]."

Worked example (course on client proposals):
"By the end of this lesson, you'll have a single-sentence value statement for your services that you can put on your website, in your proposals, and at the start of every sales call. It takes ten minutes to write using the template we'll build together. This is the part most consultants skip, which is exactly why clients keep asking 'but what do you actually do?'"

---

### Formula 4: The relatable moment

"You know that feeling when [SPECIFIC SITUATION THE AUDIENCE RECOGNIZES]? That's exactly what this lesson is for."

Worked example (course on hiring for small businesses):
"You know that feeling when you've just posted a job listing and you get 80 applications in 48 hours, and suddenly the job of finding the right person has turned into a full-time job in itself? That's exactly what this lesson is for. We're going to cut that 80 down to the three worth interviewing in under an hour."

## 3. Explanation patterns

When a concept is hard to explain, reach for one of these patterns before restating the definition differently.

### Pattern 1: The ELI5 (explain like I'm five)

Explain the concept as simply as possible, as if the student has never heard the category before. Then add the nuance.

"A profit margin is just the percentage of every dollar you earn that you actually keep after paying for everything. So if you charge $100 and it costs you $70 to deliver, you keep $30. That's a 30% margin. The tricky part is knowing what counts as 'it costs you,' which is what we'll get into."

### Pattern 2: The analogy

Connect the new concept to something the student already knows.

"Think of your content calendar like a restaurant's menu. You don't decide what to cook from scratch every day based on what's in the fridge; you decide on your menu in advance, order the right ingredients, and then focus on execution. The content calendar is the menu. It means you're always prepped, never staring at a blank screen at 7 a.m."

### Pattern 3: The contrast

Show what the concept is not, alongside what it is.

"There's a difference between a goal and a metric. A goal is where you want to end up: 'I want to grow my email list.' A metric is the number that tells you whether you're on track: 'I'm gaining 50 new subscribers per week.' The confusion between these two is why most content strategies fail to improve, even when people are working hard. We're going to be very precise about which one we're talking about."

### Pattern 4: The example-first

Give the example before the explanation, then extract the principle.

"[Example first.] Imagine a course creator who has been teaching beginner photography for two years. She notices she gets the exact same five questions in every workshop. Instead of answering them one by one, she creates a 'common mistakes' lesson that covers all five in one place. [Now extract.] What she did is called building an FAQ lesson, and it's one of the highest-impact lesson types you can include because it meets the student where they already are."

## 4. Common script failure modes

### Failure mode 1: The outline read aloud

The script sounds like bullet points being narrated. Each sentence ends with a new point; nothing flows. The student feels like they are reading a slide deck.

Fix: Rewrite each beat as a mini-story or conversation. Add transitions between sentences: "And here's why that matters...," "The reason this works is...," "Think of it this way..."

### Failure mode 2: The textbook voice

The language is formal, passive, and academic. "It is important to note that..." and "This concept can be understood by considering..." are symptoms.

Fix: Read the script aloud. If you would not say it in a conversation, change it. Replace passive constructions with active ones. Replace "it is important to" with "here's why this matters."

### Failure mode 3: The vague example

"For example, imagine a small business owner who improves their marketing." This example teaches nothing because it has no specificity.

Fix: Name the fictional person. Give them a situation, a constraint, a decision, and a result. "Imagine Marcus, a freelance copywriter three years into his business. He's been charging $500 per project. He applies the value-framing method from Lesson 4 to his next proposal and increases his rate to $1,200. The client says yes on the first call. Here's exactly how Marcus framed the conversation."

### Failure mode 4: The missing action step

The lesson ends with a summary and then nothing. The student has consumed content but has not done anything.

Fix: Every lesson ends with one specific instruction that produces visible output. It cannot be "think about what you learned." It must be a task with a product: a written answer, a completed section, a made decision, an attempt at the skill.

### Failure mode 5: The overlong beat

One beat runs for four minutes and covers three distinct ideas. The student loses track of the main point.

Fix: Each beat covers one idea. If a beat is running long, split it. The test: can you state the point of this beat in one sentence? If not, it is more than one beat.
