# Action Step and Example Bank

Plug-and-play action step formats, fully written anchor-example patterns, and calibration notes by student level.

---

## 1. Thirty action step formats by lesson type

### Concept lessons

1. "Write a one-sentence definition of [CONCEPT] in your own words, without looking at the lesson notes."
2. "Find one example of [CONCEPT] in your own work or life from the past week. Describe it in two to three sentences."
3. "List the three most important things you learned in this lesson and rank them by which one will have the most impact on your work."
4. "Draw a simple diagram of [FRAMEWORK] by hand. Label each part. Keep it rough; it's for your own understanding."
5. "Identify which step of [PROCESS] you are currently at. Write one sentence on what that means for your next action."

### Demonstration lessons

6. "Pause the video after each step in the demonstration and try the step yourself on your own [MATERIAL/PROJECT] before watching the next."
7. "Complete [STEP 1] of the demonstrated process on your own [PROJECT]. Screenshot or save your output before the next lesson."
8. "Identify one decision the instructor made in the demonstration that surprised you. Write down what you would have done instead and why you think the instructor's approach is better."
9. "Replicate the demonstration on a practice version of your own work. It does not need to be perfect; it needs to be started."
10. "Complete the entire process demonstrated in the lesson on a simplified version of your own content or project."

### Worked example lessons

11. "Apply [KEY PRINCIPLE FROM THE EXAMPLE] to one piece of your own work. Write down what you changed and why."
12. "Compare the strong example and your current version of [ARTIFACT]. List three specific differences."
13. "Identify the single biggest gap between the worked example and your current approach. Write one sentence on how to close it."
14. "Use the worked example as a checklist. Mark each element your current [ARTIFACT] does or does not include."
15. "Rewrite one section of your own [ARTIFACT] using the approach shown in the worked example."

### Practice lessons

16. "Complete the [EXERCISE] in the worksheet provided. Set a timer for [TIME]. Do not edit as you go; just produce."
17. "Write your first draft of [DELIVERABLE] without stopping to second-guess. Aim for done, not perfect."
18. "Complete [TASK] using the template in your workbook. If you get stuck, refer back to Beat 3 of this lesson."
19. "Produce one [OUTPUT TYPE] before the next lesson. Share it in the course community if you want feedback."
20. "Make the decision this lesson asked you to make. Write it down and commit to it for at least the next two weeks."

### Synthesis lessons

21. "Complete [MODULE DELIVERABLE] in full using all the methods covered in this module. This is your completion marker for Module [N]."
22. "Review your synthesis output against the quality checklist in the workbook. Note any gaps. You do not need to fix them tonight; just name them."
23. "Compare your synthesis output with the worked example in the example file. What is the most significant difference? Write one sentence."
24. "Save your completed [DELIVERABLE] in your course folder. You'll use it in Module [N+1]."
25. "Share your completed [DELIVERABLE] with one person who matches your target audience. Ask them to tell you one thing that is unclear. Note their answer."

### Reflection and planning lessons

26. "Write your answer to this question in under five minutes: [REFLECTION QUESTION SPECIFIC TO THE LESSON CONTENT]."
27. "Fill in the planning template for [TOPIC]. Do not overthink it; a rough version is better than a blank page."
28. "Set a calendar reminder for [SPECIFIC TASK] based on what you decided in this lesson."
29. "Write a brief message to future-you, explaining the decision you made today and why."
30. "Identify one thing from this lesson you can apply today, not next week. Name it. Do it."

## 2. Five fully written anchor-example patterns

These patterns can be adapted for any topic. Replace the [BRACKET] items with content-specific details.

### Pattern A: The before-and-after case

Fictional persona: [FIRST NAME], a [ROLE] with [EXPERIENCE LEVEL] who was struggling with [SPECIFIC PROBLEM].

Before state: "[FIRST NAME] was [doing/saying/creating X]. The result was always [NEGATIVE OUTCOME]. They tried [PRIOR ATTEMPT] but it did not help because [REASON]."

The change: "[FIRST NAME] applied [METHOD FROM THE LESSON]. Specifically, they [concrete action with detail]. The key decision was [HIGHLIGHT THE MOST IMPORTANT CHOICE THEY MADE]."

After state: "The result: [SPECIFIC, REALISTIC OUTCOME WITH A NUMBER OR CONCRETE CHANGE IF POSSIBLE]. More importantly, [SECOND-ORDER BENEFIT: what this made easier, faster, or possible that wasn't before]."

Transferable principle: "What made this work was not [common misconception] but [the actual mechanism]. That is what we will practice in the next lesson."

---

**Fully written example for a course on project management:**

Fictional persona: Jordan, a marketing manager at a 12-person agency, three years into a career that has moved fast but feels disorganized.

Before: Jordan was starting every project by opening a blank Google Doc and writing everything down in whatever order it occurred. Every project felt different. Deadlines slipped because tasks fell through the gaps. They had tried keeping a personal to-do list, but projects involve other people, and the list was always missing something.

The change: Jordan applied the project kickoff template from Lesson 2.3. Specifically, they spent 30 minutes filling out the scope section and listing every deliverable they could think of. The key decision was forcing themselves to write "who owns this" next to every deliverable before the kickoff meeting.

After: The project launched on schedule for the first time in four months. More importantly, the team stopped asking Jordan "what should I be doing?" every morning, because the ownership column answered that question before it was asked.

Transferable principle: What made this work was not a fancier tool but a single forced decision: every deliverable needs an owner before the project starts. That is the one habit the template installs.

---

### Pattern B: The two-path contrast

Show two approaches to the same situation. One follows the method from the lesson; one follows the common default.

"Here's the same situation, handled two different ways.

[FIRST NAME A] [does X the default way]. [SHOW THE SPECIFIC LANGUAGE, DECISION, OR APPROACH.] The result: [REALISTIC NEGATIVE OUTCOME].

[FIRST NAME B] [does X the method way]. [SHOW THE SPECIFIC LANGUAGE, DECISION, OR APPROACH.] The result: [REALISTIC POSITIVE OUTCOME].

The difference is not [common assumption about why one is better]. It is [THE ACTUAL REASON FROM THE LESSON CONTENT]."

---

### Pattern C: The zoomed-in decision

Zoom into one specific moment or decision within a process to show exactly what the method looks like in practice.

"Let's zoom in on the moment that usually goes wrong.

Imagine [FIRST NAME], who is [IN SITUATION]. They have to [MAKE DECISION / TAKE ACTION]. Most people in this situation [DO X]. Here's what that looks like in practice: [VERBATIM OR NEAR-VERBATIM EXAMPLE OF THE DEFAULT].

The problem with that is [SPECIFIC REASON].

Here's what it looks like when you apply [METHOD]: [VERBATIM OR NEAR-VERBATIM EXAMPLE OF THE METHOD].

Same situation. Completely different signal to the person on the other end."

---

### Pattern D: The worked number

For quantitative or analytical lessons, walk through a complete calculation or analysis with a specific, realistic example.

"Let's work through a real example. [FIRST NAME] runs [TYPE OF BUSINESS]. Their numbers are:

[METRIC A]: [VALUE]
[METRIC B]: [VALUE]
[METRIC C]: [VALUE]

Step 1 of the [METHOD]: [APPLY THE FIRST STEP TO THE NUMBERS]. This gives us [RESULT A].

Step 2: [APPLY THE SECOND STEP]. This gives us [RESULT B].

What does [RESULT B] tell us? It tells us [INTERPRETATION IN PLAIN LANGUAGE]. For [FIRST NAME], this means [CONCRETE IMPLICATION FOR THEIR SITUATION].

The number we want to keep an eye on going forward is [KEY METRIC]. If it drops below [THRESHOLD], the action is [RESPONSE]."

---

### Pattern E: The step-by-step with narrated decisions

For process lessons, walk through each step with explicit narration of why each decision is made.

"Here's [FIRST NAME] going through [PROCESS] step by step.

Step 1: [FIRST NAME] starts by [ACTION]. The reason they start here rather than [ALTERNATIVE STARTING POINT] is [REASON].

Step 2: [ACTION]. Notice that they [SPECIFIC DETAIL]. That matters because [REASON].

Step 3: [ACTION]. This is where most people [MAKE A COMMON ERROR]. [FIRST NAME] avoids it by [SPECIFIC BEHAVIOR].

The finished output: [DESCRIBE THE COMPLETED ARTIFACT OR RESULT]. The whole process took [REALISTIC TIME ESTIMATE].

If [FIRST NAME] had [MADE THE COMMON ERROR FROM STEP 3], the output would have looked like [CONTRAST]. The extra minute in Step 3 is what prevented that."

## 3. Calibration notes by student level

### Beginner students

Action steps should produce visible output in under 20 minutes. Avoid open-ended reflection prompts; beginners need concrete tasks with clear success criteria. Use "complete this template" and "write your answer to this specific question" rather than "reflect on your experience."

Examples should use everyday vocabulary without jargon. If a technical term is used in the example, define it within the example itself, not in a separate lesson note.

Keep examples small-scale and low-stakes: a beginner course on running a business should use examples about a business with one to two employees, not a 20-person team.

### Intermediate students

Action steps can be more open-ended. "Apply the method to your own [PROJECT]" is appropriate when the student has enough foundation to make decisions. Offer optional stretch tasks for students who want to go further.

Examples can include industry-specific vocabulary without definition. Use more complex scenarios that reflect the real challenges the intermediate student faces (competing priorities, imperfect information, time constraints).

### Advanced students

Action steps should push toward edge cases and novel applications. "Apply the method and then identify one situation where it would fail. What would you do instead?" is the right level of challenge.

Examples should include failure cases, exceptions, and trade-offs, not just successful clean applications of the method.
